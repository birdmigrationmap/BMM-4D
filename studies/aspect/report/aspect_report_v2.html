Bird aspect correction for weather-radar density

A bird seen side-on reflects more radar energy than the same bird seen head-on or tail-on. When migrants share a flight direction, this becomes an azimuthal pattern in radar bird density that does not reflect where the birds are. This report explains the effect, why a single scan cannot separate it from real structure, what the current correction (Appendix B) does, and how radar overlaps measure it with the density field cancelled, which leads to one shared aspect function.

DataEvery fitted number comes from one autumn night, 21–22 October 2026: 107 NEXRAD radars of the us_east domain, 26 frames from 23:30 to 12:00 UTC.

1Why aspect matters

This section explains the physical cause: the cross-section of one bird depends on the viewing angle, and averaging over a population with scattered headings sets the amplitude a weather radar sees.

Density is reflectivity divided by an assumed cross-section per bird. If the cross-section depends on the viewing angle \(\psi = \phi - \theta\), the derived density is the true density times \(T(\psi)\), the cross-section at \(\psi\) relative to its mean over all angles:

\[ \hat\rho(r,\phi) = \rho(r,\phi)\,T(\psi), \qquad \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} T(\psi)\,d\psi = 1 . \]

Single birds held with wings closed are strongly anisotropic: at X band, broadside is 14–90 times head-on and head-on 1.1–1.4 times tail-on (Edwards & Houghton 1959; Konrad et al. 1968). Flight weakens this and can reverse it. A bat with spread wings reflects less side-on than head-on at X band (side/head 0.54, against about 4 for a wingless body model; Mirkovic et al. 2016). Wing flapping makes the cross-section fluctuate by at least 10 dB (Gong et al. 2022), and terns in flight show broadside only 3–4 dB above head and tail (Urmy & Warren 2017). At S band passerines are near resonance, where bird reflectivity can change by more than 10 dB with frequency (Melnikov et al. 2012). We found no published S-band side/head ratio for passerines in flight. Weather-radar studies note lobes of higher density across the flight (Kranstauber et al. 2020). Standard tools do not correct them (Dokter et al. 2011, 2019); Hoekstra et al. (2025) remove a 180°-period sine fitted to each scan, close to the per-volume approach of Appendix B.

Headings scatter around \(\theta\). Writing one bird's pattern as \(T_1(\psi) = 1 + \alpha_1\cos\psi - \alpha_2\cos 2\psi + \dots\) and headings as \(\delta \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\), the radar sees the average over \(\delta\), and each harmonic \(n\) is damped by \(\mathbb E[\cos n\delta] = e^{-n^2\sigma^2/2}\):

\[ T(\psi) = \mathbb E_\delta\!\left[T_1(\psi-\delta)\right] = 1 + \alpha_1 e^{-\sigma^2/2}\cos\psi - \alpha_2 e^{-2\sigma^2}\cos 2\psi + \dots \]

The side-on term keeps 58% of its single-bird amplitude at \(\sigma = 30^\circ\) and 29% at \(45^\circ\); the front/back term keeps 87% and 73%. A population side-on amplitude of 0.05–0.3 is therefore plausible at S band, together with a front/back term. The rest of the report uses the log form, where \(c_2 \approx \alpha_2\) and \(c_1 \approx \alpha_1\) for small amplitudes and \(N\) keeps the mean of \(T\) at 1:

\[ \log T(\psi) = c_2\,(-\cos 2\psi) + c_1\cos\psi - \log N . \]

2What one radar sees

This section shows why one scan cannot separate aspect from the density field: in the log, both add azimuthal harmonics, and real fields can produce exactly the aspect ones.

\[ \log\hat\rho(r,\phi) = \log\rho(r,\phi) \;+\; c_2\,\bigl(-\cos 2(\phi-\theta)\bigr) \;+\; c_1\cos(\phi-\theta) \;-\; \log N . \]

Aspect adds a second harmonic peaking side-on and a first harmonic along the flight axis, both at a fixed phase. The field can add any harmonic. A log-density gradient that increases upstream has the form of \(c_1 < 0\); a band of birds through the radar, across the flight, has the form of \(c_2 > 0\). A scan shows only the sum. Range gives little leverage, because the beam rises with range and the aspect amplitude grows with height.

The night's volumes agree. Aspect is symmetric about the flight axis and makes no harmonic at 45° to it, yet at 10–60 km the per-volume spread of the side-on harmonic equals that of the 45° harmonic (standard deviations 0.145 and 0.145). In a single volume the side-on harmonic is mostly field structure.

3The Appendix B method

This section summarises the correction of Appendix B, which production applies now, and reproduces its fit on the synthetic scan to show what it removes.

  1. Axis. A sinusoid \(q(\phi) = A\sin(\phi+\varphi) + C\) is fitted to the density-weighted azimuthal mean of ρHV, pooled over sweeps. For birds, ρHV is lowest tail-on and highest head-on (Stepanian & Horton 2015); for insects the pattern can flip between radars with a different differential phase on transmission (Stepanian et al. 2016; Melnikov & Bridge 2024).
  2. Profile. Per sweep, a trimmed mean over range, smoothed in azimuth, gives \(m_e(\phi)\).
  3. Template. An asymmetric ellipse \(R(\phi)\) is fitted to the pooled \(m_e(\phi)\), weighted by \(m_e\).
  4. Correction. Each sweep is divided by \(R(\phi)\) and rescaled to its own mean.
\[ R(\phi) = x_m\,\frac{a\,b(\psi_B)}{\sqrt{a^2\sin^2\psi_B + b(\psi_B)^2\cos^2\psi_B}}, \qquad b(\psi_B) = \begin{cases} b_1, & \psi_B \bmod 360^\circ < 180^\circ \\ b_2, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Notation. Appendix B measures \(\psi_B = \phi - \theta_B\) from the fitted axis \(\theta_B\). \(R\) equals \(x_m a\) at \(\psi_B = 0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\), which the appendix calls broadside, so \(\theta_B\) lies 90° from the flight axis \(\theta\) used here. In the flight frame, \(R = x_m\,a\,b(\psi)\big/\sqrt{a^2\cos^2\psi + b(\psi)^2\sin^2\psi}\): side-on gets \(x_m a\), and the two along-track directions get \(x_m b_1\) and \(x_m b_2\). Production fits \(a\) and a share \(\beta\), with \(b_1 = \beta/a\) and \(b_2 = (1-\beta)/a\).

Because \(R(\phi)\) is fitted to the scan it corrects, it absorbs any second harmonic on the broadside axis and any first harmonic along the flight axis, whatever their cause. A band across the flight is removed together with the aspect, and the rescaling moves density between sectors, which gives a bow-tie on a map (Figure 3). On this night the ellipse leaves c₂ 0.073 and c₁ −0.116 on the overlap measurement (from 0.200 and −0.220), and it lowers the median first harmonic of single scans at 10–60 km from 0.27 to 0.20, which is field structure.

4The overlap principle

This section derives a measurement of T in which the density cancels: two radars that see the same cell at the same time see the same birds from two angles. It ends with the regression the study uses.

\[ \log\hat\rho_{A,j} - \log\hat\rho_{B,j} = \log T(\psi_{A,j}) - \log T(\psi_{B,j}) + \log\frac{k_A}{k_B} + \varepsilon_j . \]

Here \(\psi_A = \phi_A - \theta\) and \(\psi_B = \phi_B - \theta\). The true density cancels, and so does \(\log N\), so the overlap identifies the shape of \(T\). The terms \(k_A, k_B\) (calibration, per-volume scale, beam smoothing) do not depend on the angles. Cells where one radar sees the birds side-on and the other head-on or tail-on carry the most information (Figure 4).

Over all cells seen by two radars, the study fits

\[ y_j = \sum_{n=1}^{2} c_n\bigl[g_n(\psi_{A,j}) - g_n(\psi_{B,j})\bigr] + \gamma_{AB,t,\ell} + \lambda_\ell \log\frac{r_{A,j}}{r_{B,j}} + \varepsilon_j, \]

with \(g_1 = \cos\psi\) and \(g_2 = -\cos 2\psi\), a fixed effect \(\gamma\) per radar pair, frame and 500 m layer for calibration, scale and beam smoothing, a log range-ratio slope \(\lambda\) per layer for beam broadening, and errors clustered by radar pair. Cells are 4 × 4 km × 100 m, moved to the frame time with each radar's velocity profile. A version with no imposed shape uses 24 bins of 15° instead of the two harmonics. On this night the data are 2.96 million cell pairs from 197 radar pairs, at 0.8–3.5 km above the antennas.

5The shared function

This section defines the function proposed from the overlap measurement, then how it is applied to a volume without fitting anything to that volume.

The 24-bin curve has its minimum tail-on, two equal side-on maxima and head-on in between (Figure 5). Its harmonics are c₂ = 0.200 ± 0.012 and c₁ = −0.208 ± 0.019; the fourth harmonic (0.020 ± 0.009) and the terms that symmetry sets to zero are small, so two harmonics suffice. c₂ grows with height, from 0.07 at 0.75–1 km to 0.24 at 2.5–3.5 km, while c₁ stays between −0.16 and −0.25 above 1 km. The proposed function is

\[ \log T(\psi; h) = c_2(h)\,(-\cos 2\psi) + c_1(h)\cos\psi - \log N(h), \qquad \psi = \phi - \theta(h), \]
\[ c_2(h) = 0.092\,h, \qquad c_1(h) = -0.21\,\min(1,\,h), \qquad h \text{ in km above the antenna}, \]

with c₂ = 0.092 ± 0.005 per km and c₁ = −0.21 ± 0.02, the same in both folds. A constant c₂ over-corrects near the radar, so the amplitude has to grow with height. The ramp of c₁ below 1 km is a modelling choice: without it, the near-range first harmonic of single scans turns around (+0.09, against +0.01 with the ramp). \(N(h)\) makes the azimuthal mean of \(T\) equal to 1 at every height, so a uniform field keeps its mean and no per-sweep rescaling is needed:

\[ N(h) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} \exp\!\Bigl(c_2(h)\,(-\cos 2\psi) + c_1(h)\cos\psi\Bigr)\,d\psi \;\approx\; \exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{c_2^2 + c_1^2}{4}\Bigr) . \]

The correction is applied per gate. The beam-centre height \(h\) above the antenna (4/3 earth radius) gives the track \(\theta(h)\) from the volume's own velocity profile (100 m layers; empty layers take the nearest one), then \(T(\psi; h)\); the density and its standard deviation are divided by \(T\). Nothing is fitted to the volume, so real gradients pass through.

6Worked example: KDMX, 06:30 UTC on 22 October 2026

This section follows one volume through the shared function and compares the result with the ellipse, on the maps and on the azimuthal profile.

The track at KDMX turns with height: 63° in the lowest 100 m, 133° at 500–600 m, about 186° at 1.6–1.9 km and 160° at 3 km, with speed rising from 5 to 12.7 m s⁻¹. The three lowest sweeps reach 0.3–0.7 km above the antenna at 30 km and 1.2–2.5 km at 90 km. At a fixed azimuth, ψ therefore changes with range, which twists the factor map.

Within 90 km on the 0.5–1.3° sweeps, the shared factor runs from 0.82 to 1.49 (1st–99th percentile of gates). It is largest downstream, where birds are seen tail-on, and aloft. The dense band NW of the radar and the low density to the S–SE are kept. The ellipse factor runs from 0.66 to 2.9 by azimuth: it lifts the S–SE sector and lowers the NW band, the bow-tie of Figure 3 on real data. KDMX's deepest 10° polar sector over 04:30–07:30 UTC, relative to its ±30° neighbours, is 0.87 without correction, 0.83 with the shared function and 0.72 with the ellipse.

7Results

This section reports three tests briefly: the measured function, the out-of-sample disagreement between overlapping radars, and a reconstruction with held-out radars.

Measured function. Relative to the azimuthal mean, tail-on is 0.70, side-on 1.29 and head-on 1.01. The amplitudes hold for equal ranges with matched beams (c₂ 0.209) and through the night (c₂ 0.18–0.20, c₁ −0.19 to −0.22). ZV is modulated as much as ZH (c₂ 0.227 and 0.225), and c₁ differs between them (−0.15 and −0.32), which points to the scatterers rather than radar processing. After the shared correction the overlap curve is flat (Figure 7).

8Limits

This section lists what one night cannot settle. Each point comes from the study's own checks.

  • One autumn night (21–22 October 2026, us_east, 107 radars), with tracks mostly toward the S. No spring night was tested.
  • Aspect or fixed radar patterns. Partial blockage, sector calibration and lasting gradients are fixed in each radar's geographic frame. Fitted together with a fixed azimuthal pattern per radar, c₂ falls from 0.092 to 0.017 per km and c₁ from −0.21 to −0.06. With one track direction the two cannot be told apart: one night cannot decide, and the joint fit is no evidence that the aspect is small. For the same reason, per-radar amplitudes (sd 0.24, reproducible between folds) are not used.
  • Below about 1 km, where most birds fly, the model is an extrapolation. The overlap starts at 0.8 km with few pairs below 1.25 km, and the 1 km ramp of c₁ is a choice.
  • Axis. The side-on pattern does not turn with the track during the night, and on the overlap the axis lies between the track and a fixed preferred heading. A low-level track (200–1500 m) halves the amplitude. The ρHV heading matched the track at the cell height in the 2 frames tested. Headings differ from tracks by 34° on average at inland radars (Horton et al. 2016); if the axis is the heading, a track-based fit sees the side-on term reduced by the mean of cos 2Δ over the heading–track angles Δ.
  • The amplitude varies by region (c₂ 0.28, 0.13 and 0.12 from west to east) and with ground speed (0.06 below 6 m s⁻¹, 0.24 at 10–14 m s⁻¹). Slow echoes may include insects, which have their own side-on pattern and often another axis (Riley 1985; Chapman et al. 2010). Above 3 km the linear c₂ overshoots (0.30 against 0.24).

Whether the parameters must be refitted for each night or can form one climatological function is open.
